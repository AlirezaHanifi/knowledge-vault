Small files and compaction

A streaming job that writes every few minutes creates a new small file each time. Every reader then pays a fixed cost per file, for listing and opening it, on top of the cost of reading the data. Compaction merges small files into big ones. It costs compute itself, and the replaced files stay on storage until a vacuum removes them.

Illustrates “Pattern: Compactor” (pp. 27–30). The book's scenario is a sync job writing to an object store, with batch jobs spending 70% of their time listing files after three months. The cost model here is the vault's and only illustrative: one 2 MB file per 10-minute micro-batch (144 per day), 50 ms of overhead per file, 10 ms per MB processed and 128 MB compacted files. With these numbers, an uncompacted table spends about 70% of its time on files. A daily batch job reads the whole table. Vacuum removes replaced files older than the retention period. Implementation is the vault's own.

    What to notice

    • Without compaction, file overhead dominates. The red share of the batch job stays around 70%, and the job keeps getting slower as files pile up.
    • Rare compaction is cheap but leaves gaps. With weekly compaction, readers face up to a week of small files between runs. Daily compaction keeps that to the current day.
    • Compaction alone doesn't free storage. Turn off vacuum and the replaced files stay. With vacuum on, you can no longer restore versions older than the retention period.
