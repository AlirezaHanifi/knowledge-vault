Innovation tokens

In Dan McKinley’s model, which the book quotes, a company gets about three innovation tokens, and every technology choice that isn’t boring spends one. Choose a stack for April’s team and see where your tokens go: on what differentiates the business, or on making authentication 30 ms faster.

Illustrates “Scaling Without Overengineering” and “Choose Boring Technology”, pp. 158–162, including April’s database consolidation (p. 159) and Rust proposal (p. 162). The choices come from the book; their token costs follow McKinley’s examples and the authors’ update (Kubernetes and service meshes now count) — the token prices for the five-database mix and a homegrown database are the vault’s.

    What to notice

    • Boring isn’t bad. PostgreSQL, Python and cron are boring and good — you already know how they fail.
    • April spends her tokens where customers notice: the animation rendering engine and real-time collaboration. That leaves nothing for a Rust rewrite customers never asked for.
    • Every non-boring choice brings operational overhead, a smaller hiring pool and more for juniors to learn — the cost isn’t paid once, it’s paid every day.
    • A new technology should create measurable value — lower costs, faster development, a bigger market, happier customers or new revenue. If you can’t say which, you’re probably spending a token unwisely.
