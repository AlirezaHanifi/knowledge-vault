How long before a node is dead?

A monitor hears a heartbeat from a node every 100 ms. Silence might mean the node crashed — or only that a packet sat in a queue or the process paused for garbage collection. The timeout decides: too short and live nodes are declared dead, too long and a real crash goes unnoticed.

Illustrates “Fault Detection”, “Timeouts and Unbounded Delays” and “Network congestion and queueing” (pp. 351–355): unbounded delays, the 2d + r bound that real networks don’t give you, and automatically adjusted timeouts such as the Phi Accrual detector. What to notice

  • No safe value. Every timeout trades the two charts against each other; you can only move along the curve.
  • Pauses and queueing dominate. Raise the pause rate or the queueing delay: the gaps get a long tail and short timeouts start declaring a live node dead.
  • A false “dead” is costly. The node may still be doing work while another takes over (an action can run twice), and moving its load can tip an overloaded system into cascading failure (p. 352).
  • Measure, then adapt. The book suggests choosing timeouts experimentally from observed round-trip times, or adjusting them automatically as Phi Accrual does (p. 355).
